Indian agriculture runs on heavily subsidised — and often diesel — power for irrigation. **PM-KUSUM** (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan) is the scheme trying to change that by putting solar at the heart of the farm.

## Three schemes in one

PM-KUSUM has **three components**:

- **Component A:** small, decentralised grid-connected solar plants (up to 2 MW) on farmers' barren or fallow land, selling power to the local utility.
- **Component B:** **standalone, off-grid solar pumps** for farms without a grid connection, replacing diesel.
- **Component C:** **solarising existing grid-connected pumps** and whole agricultural **feeders**, so irrigation runs on daytime solar.

The scheme's original ambition was about **34,800 MW** of solar capacity, backed by roughly **₹34,422 crore** in central financial support.

## Where it stands

Progress has been lopsided. **Standalone pumps (Component B)** and **feeder solarisation (Component C)** have advanced most, together reaching well over a million pumps; by 2026 the government reported that around **21.77 lakh farmers** had benefited across the scheme. **Component A**, the small farm-gate plants, has lagged well behind its target, held back by land, grid-connection and offtake complexities. The Union budget for 2026-27 allocated around **₹5,000 crore** to keep the scheme moving, and a **Phase II** has been flagged to carry it forward.

## Why it matters

Solarising irrigation does three things at once: it cuts diesel use and emissions, it reduces the enormous electricity subsidies states pay for farm power, and it hands farmers either free daytime pumping or a new revenue stream from selling surplus power. Done at scale, it also shifts a big, flexible load — pumping — into the **midday solar window**, exactly when the grid has surplus to spare.

It is one of the rare clean-energy programmes that touches livelihoods, state finances and grid balancing all at once.
