India's best wind sites — the ridgelines of Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Maharashtra — were colonised early, in the 1990s and 2000s, by small turbines of a few hundred kilowatts. Those machines occupy prime wind resource but generate a fraction of what a modern turbine on the same spot could. Fixing that mismatch is the idea behind **repowering**.

## The opportunity

The National Institute of Wind Energy has estimated India's repowering potential at roughly **25 GW**, concentrated in turbines rated **under 2 MW**. The arithmetic is compelling: a single modern turbine of 3–5 MW, with a far taller tower and larger rotor, can produce several times the energy of the sub-megawatt machines it replaces — on land that already has grid connectivity, access roads and consent.

## The policy

MNRE issued a **National Repowering and Life Extension Policy** in 2023, replacing a narrower 2016 framework, to make these projects easier to execute, with state nodal agencies handling intra-state projects and a central agency for inter-state ones. In 2026, the government signalled it is formulating a fuller strategy to repower around **25 GW** — explicitly tied to the national goal of **100 GW of wind by 2030**, up from roughly 58–59 GW installed in mid-2026.

## Why it's harder than it sounds

Repowering is not simply bolting on a bigger turbine. Old sites have fragmented land leases and existing power purchase agreements at outdated tariffs; owners need certainty that a new, larger machine can be connected and sold profitably. Financing, grid-connectivity rules and the economics of decommissioning old turbines all have to line up.

Done well, repowering is some of the cheapest clean capacity India can add — recycling its best wind real estate instead of hunting for new sites. Done slowly, it leaves gigawatts of prime wind underused.
