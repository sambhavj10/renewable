India's solar manufacturing story is usually told as a triumph — capacity soaring past 200 GW. But that headline hides where the real dependence lives: **upstream**, in the polysilicon, ingots and wafers that every panel starts with, and that India barely makes.

## The lopsided chain

Trace a solar panel back through its supply chain and India's capacity collapses at each step:

- **Modules:** well above **200 GW** a year — comfortably self-sufficient.
- **Cells:** above **30 GW** and climbing fast.
- **Ingots and wafers:** only around **2 GW**.
- **Polysilicon:** effectively **nil**.

According to figures attributed to NITI Aayog, India's **polysilicon import dependence is close to 100%**, and **wafer dependence above 90%** — overwhelmingly on China. So even a cell stamped "made in India" is usually sliced from an imported wafer grown from imported polysilicon.

## The policy response

The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy has been working on a **production-linked incentive scheme for polysilicon**, with an initial scope of more than **10 GW** and a target of at least **30 GW by 2030**. The government has also floated a goal of at least **80 GW of ingot and wafer capacity by June 2028**. Because polysilicon also feeds the semiconductor industry, officials have weighed treating it as a distinct strategic sector.

## Why it's so hard

Upstream plants are brutally capital-intensive — an integrated polysilicon-to-cell facility can cost **$150–350 million per GW** of annual capacity — and highly technical, with over 90% of the specialised tooling (Siemens reactors, ingot furnaces, diamond-wire saws) itself imported. Polysilicon is also a commodity where Chinese producers hold a vast scale-and-cost advantage.

Closing this gap is the difference between assembling panels and truly owning the solar supply chain. It is the hardest, most expensive part of India's manufacturing ambition — and the one that matters most.
