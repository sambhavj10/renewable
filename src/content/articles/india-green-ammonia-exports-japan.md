Hydrogen is hard to ship. Ammonia — three hydrogen atoms bound to one nitrogen — is not: the world already moves it by the tanker-load for fertiliser. That is why **green ammonia**, made with renewable-powered electrolysis instead of fossil gas, has become India's most tangible route to both cleaning up fertiliser and **exporting its cheap solar and wind** as a tradable fuel.

## The domestic anchor

India consumes vast quantities of ammonia for fertiliser, almost all of it made from natural gas. To start substituting it, the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI) ran a landmark tender under the green hydrogen mission to supply around **724,000 tonnes a year** of green ammonia across **13 fertiliser plants**, backed by long-term offtake agreements of about a decade. Guaranteed domestic demand like this is what makes first projects financeable.

## The export story

The more eye-catching deals point outward — chiefly to **Japan**. **ACME**, awarded **370,000 tonnes a year** of capacity in the SECI process, has signed an offtake arrangement with **IHI** for roughly **405,000 tonnes a year** of green ammonia, supported by Japan's contract-for-difference scheme for low-carbon fuels, plus a green methanol deal with Mitsubishi Gas Chemical. **NTPC** executed a green ammonia purchase agreement and has said it is in talks with buyers in **Japan, South Korea and Germany**.

## The hurdle is cost

Green ammonia still costs more than the fossil-based product, and export projects live or die on firm, long-dated offtake and the buying country's willingness to pay a premium (or subsidise it, as Japan is doing). India's advantages — abundant land, falling renewable tariffs and ambitious policy — make it a natural low-cost producer, but turning awards into shipped tonnes is a multi-year build.

If it works, ammonia could become the vessel that carries Indian sunshine to the world.
