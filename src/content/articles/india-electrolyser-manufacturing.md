Every kilogram of green hydrogen starts in an **electrolyser** — the machine that splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using renewable electricity. India has decided it does not want to import that machine the way it imported solar cells. Under the National Green Hydrogen Mission, it is building a domestic **electrolyser manufacturing** base from close to zero.

## The incentives

Through the **SIGHT** programme, India has awarded incentives covering about **3,000 MW a year** of electrolyser manufacturing capacity to **15 companies**, backed by roughly **₹4,440 crore**, across two tender tranches of 1.5 GW each. Demand to participate has been strong: the second tranche was **oversubscribed**, drawing bids for over 3,300 MW against a 1,500 MW target, with eleven firms receiving letters of award.

## Awarded is not built

The crucial caveat is that **awarded capacity is not operating capacity**. The clearest large operating facility is **Ohmium's** plant near Bengaluru, with roughly **2 GW a year** of capacity and room to expand. Others are building: **Waaree** broke ground on a 300 MW electrolyser facility in Gujarat, and **Advait**, via a technology transfer, plans 300 MW rising to 1,000 MW. Most of the 3,000 MW remains a pipeline rather than a running factory floor.

## Why it matters

Electrolysers are the pick-and-shovel business of the hydrogen economy. If India can make them cheaply and at scale — across alkaline and the newer PEM technologies — it lowers the cost of every green hydrogen and green ammonia project downstream, and it avoids swapping dependence on imported fossil fuels for dependence on imported clean-tech hardware. The International Solar Alliance has projected India could reach **15–17 GW a year** of electrolyser capacity by 2030.

The ambition is clear. The work now is converting letters of award into operating lines.
