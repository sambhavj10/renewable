For years the knock on renewables was simple: the sun sets and the wind drops. India's answer is a tender design that writes reliability directly into the contract — **Firm and Dispatchable Renewable Energy (FDRE)**.

## How it works

An FDRE project is not a solar farm or a wind farm; it is a **package** — solar plus wind plus battery (and sometimes pumped) storage — engineered to deliver a committed amount of power when the buyer needs it. Developers build on a **build-own-operate** basis, bid for blocks typically between **100 MW and 500 MW**, and sign **25-year power purchase agreements** with the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI).

The teeth are in the performance rules. A typical round-the-clock FDRE contract requires the developer to meet a **minimum demand-fulfilment ratio of around 90% during peak hours** and high thresholds off-peak, with annual availability obligations too. Miss them, and penalties apply. In effect, the developer — not the grid — carries the job of firming intermittent output.

## The price of firmness

Firmness costs more than plain solar, but far less than it used to. SECI's **1 GW FDRE round-the-clock tender** discovered a tariff of about **₹5.25/kWh** in 2026 — remarkable for power that behaves like a conventional plant. An earlier FDRE tranche, offering 1.2 GW with 4.8 GWh of assured peak supply, cleared with a lowest bid near **₹6.27/kWh**, so prices are falling as storage gets cheaper.

## Why it matters

FDRE is the structure that lets renewables compete head-on with new coal for *baseload-like* contracts — and it is increasingly what large commercial buyers and distribution utilities want. Every FDRE award also pulls more storage onto the grid, because that is the only way to meet the delivery guarantees.

If solar auctions defined India's last decade, firm, storage-backed tenders like FDRE may define the next.
