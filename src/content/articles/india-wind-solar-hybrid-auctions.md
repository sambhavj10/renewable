A solar farm sits idle at night; a wind farm can go quiet for days. But their weak spots often don't overlap — wind frequently picks up in the evening and through the monsoon, just as solar fades. Putting both on the **same site, sharing the same grid connection**, smooths the combined output. That is the simple, powerful idea behind **wind-solar hybrid** projects.

## The economics

Hybrids make better use of expensive infrastructure. One transmission line and one substation serve two generators whose peaks fall at different times, so the connection runs closer to full capacity more of the day — a higher **capacity utilisation factor**. Add a battery, and the package starts to resemble firm power. The result shows up in tariffs: in 2026, SECI's hybrid tranches cleared at roughly **₹3.25 to ₹3.46 per kWh**, with about **1.8 GW** awarded to developers including JSW Neo Energy, ACME, Juniper Green, AMPIN and Avaada.

**ACME Solar** signed a 25-year PPA for a **300 MW** wind-solar hybrid at **₹3.25/kWh** under SECI's Hybrid Tranche IX — a price for blended, more-dispatchable renewable energy that would have been unthinkable a few years ago. Another auction, by CESC, cleared 600 MW of hybrids at around ₹3.74–3.75/kWh.

## Why it's becoming the default

As India's tenders increasingly ask for *firm* or *round-the-clock* power rather than raw megawatt-hours, the hybrid (and hybrid-plus-storage) structure is becoming the natural building block. It gives buyers steadier supply, gives developers more revenue per connection, and gives the grid a smoother profile to manage.

Pure solar tenders aren't going away. But the combination — sun, wind and increasingly a battery, all behind one meter — is quietly becoming the workhorse of India's next phase.
