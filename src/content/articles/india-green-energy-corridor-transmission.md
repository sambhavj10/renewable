A solar park in the Thar desert or a wind farm on a Gujarat ridge is worthless if its power cannot reach the cities and factories that need it. The hidden enabler of India's renewable boom is therefore **transmission** — and the flagship programme building it is the **Green Energy Corridor (GEC)**.

## What GEC-II builds

The **intra-state GEC Phase-II**, approved by the Union Cabinet, is designed to integrate and evacuate about **20 GW** of renewable energy across **seven states** — Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh. Its estimated cost is about **₹12,031 crore**, of which the Centre funds **33% (₹3,970 crore)** as financial assistance, with state transmission utilities arranging the rest.

The physical scope is substantial. The original plan called for roughly **10,750 circuit-kilometres** of transmission lines and 27,500 MVA of substation capacity; after state-requested revisions, the target was reset to about **7,574 circuit-km** of lines and **29,737 MVA** of substations.

## Reaching the hardest places

A separate **Ladakh GEC** project aims to evacuate **13 GW** of renewable energy from one of the most remote, high-altitude corners of the country, targeted for completion by around FY2029-30 at an estimated **₹20,773 crore**. These are among the most technically demanding grid projects India has attempted.

## Why it's the real bottleneck

Developers routinely cite transmission — not panels or turbines — as the constraint that stalls projects and forces curtailment when lines fill up. Corridors take years to plan, win rights-of-way for and build, so they must be laid *ahead* of the generation they serve. If transmission lags, gigawatts of clean capacity sit idle or get switched off.

The GEC programme is, in effect, India betting that the wires will be ready when the panels are. Getting that timing right is one of the quieter determinants of whether the country hits its 2030 targets.
