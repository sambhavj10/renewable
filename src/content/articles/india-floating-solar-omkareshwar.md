In a country where farmland is precious and summers are brutal, putting solar panels on water solves two problems at once: it needs no land, and the shade cuts evaporation from reservoirs that India cannot afford to lose. That logic is driving **floating solar** — "floatovoltaics" — from novelty to a serious part of the pipeline.

## The flagship projects

The largest is at the **Omkareshwar reservoir in Madhya Pradesh**, which has reached around **278 MW** operational and is designed to scale toward **600 MW**. Its first phase was built by multiple developers, including SJVN (90 MW), NHDC (88 MW) and Amp Energy (100 MW).

**NTPC's Ramagundam** plant in Telangana, at **100 MW**, has been billed as one of India's largest commissioned floating arrays, and NTPC says it saves on the order of **2,000 million litres of water a year** by reducing evaporation. NTPC has commissioned over **220 MW** of floating solar in all, including plants at Kayamkulam in Kerala (92 MW) and Simhadri in Andhra Pradesh (25 MW).

## Small today, vast tomorrow

Nationally, commissioned floating solar is still modest — on the order of **700 MW**. But the National Institute of Solar Energy has assessed India's floating solar potential at roughly **102 GWp**, spread across reservoirs, dams and industrial water bodies. That gap between built and buildable is the opportunity.

## The catch

Floating solar costs more per megawatt than ground-mounted — specialised floats, anchoring and marine-grade cabling add up — and projects can raise real questions about water ecosystems and the rights of communities who depend on a reservoir. The Omkareshwar project, for instance, has drawn scrutiny over local impacts.

Still, for a grid hungry for clean power and short on land, India's lakes and dams are becoming an obvious next frontier.
