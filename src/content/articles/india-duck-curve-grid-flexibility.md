The more solar India builds, the stranger its grid behaves in the middle of the day. Around noon, solar output floods the system and conventional plants must back down; after sunset, solar vanishes just as homes switch on and demand peaks. Plotted over 24 hours, the net load that non-solar plants must serve traces the shape energy planners call the **"duck curve"** — and in India it is getting steeper by the month.

## The numbers behind the curve

A paper from the Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister captures the shift. Between May 2023 and May 2026, the **morning ramp-down** of conventional generation — the amount of coal, gas and hydro that must be switched off to make room for midday solar — nearly **tripled, from about 18 GW to roughly 53 GW**. The **evening ramp-up** that follows sunset roughly **doubled, from about 36 GW to about 74 GW**.

## Coal can't bend fast enough

The problem is that coal, which still anchors India's grid, cannot ramp down and back up quickly or deeply. Analysis by the think-tank Ember found that the system operator often could not reduce non-solar generation enough to absorb midday solar while keeping enough coal online for the evening peak. The result is **curtailment** — clean power switched off during peak solar hours, typically between **9:00 and 15:30**.

## Storage is the obvious fix

Ember's estimate is striking: around **10 GWh of battery storage**, charging during the midday solar window and discharging in the evening, could have absorbed much of that surplus, kept coal above its minimum safe output and avoided the curtailment. That is the economic logic driving India's storage boom — and why mandates for co-located batteries and round-the-clock tenders are multiplying.

The duck curve is not a reason to slow solar. It is the signal that India's next phase of clean energy is about **flexibility** — storage, demand response and a more nimble grid — as much as about raw gigawatts.
